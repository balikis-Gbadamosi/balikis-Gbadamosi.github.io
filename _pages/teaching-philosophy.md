I still remember the moment a classmate pulled me aside after a study 
session and said, "You have a way of making things make sense." I was 
not a professor. I had no formal training. I was simply a student who 
had grown frustrated with being spoon-fed information in a system that 
rewarded memorization over meaning. Without knowing it, I had already 
begun teaching, because I had already begun to care. That moment, more 
than any classroom I have sat in since, is the foundation of my teaching 
philosophy. I believe that teaching is not simply a profession; it is a 
duty to humanity, and I intend to carry it that way.

My positionality as a sociologist, and as someone who navigated 
undergraduate studies in a system not always designed for students like 
me, shapes every value I bring into the classroom. I did not always have 
a teacher who saw me as a whole person. I experienced the weight of 
cognitive load, of coming to class carrying life alongside coursework. 
These experiences are not incidental to my teaching, they are central 
to it. I enter the classroom as a teacher of the whole student, guided 
by the belief that empathy, intentionality, and care are not soft 
additions to good pedagogy. They are the foundation of it.

My core belief is simple: everyone can learn, but how you pass on the 
information matters. Drawing on scholars like Ken Bain, whose work in 
*What the Best College Teachers Do* challenges educators to think deeply 
about learning rather than just teaching, and bell hooks, whose writing 
on love as a classroom practice gave language to what I had always 
instinctively felt, I have set aside guiding principles for my career 
in academia. I believe that teaching is an art — beautifully woven, 
deeply intentional, and something you must fall in love with in order 
to do well. The best teachers are not born; they are continuously 
becoming.

As a sociologist, I believe this discipline demands a particular kind 
of teaching, because we practice sociology every single day in our 
schools, workplaces, friendships, and in the structures we so often 
take for granted. Sociology has an explanation for the social world, 
and my goal is to help students uncover those explanations naturally. 
I want them to relearn what they thought they already knew, to see 
familiar experiences through a structural lens, and to find themselves 
doing sociology in their everyday interactions without even realizing it.

My learning goals are grounded in higher order thinking. I want students 
to move beyond surface understanding into deep comprehension, critical 
analysis, and real-world application of sociological concepts. I want 
them to leave my class with the ability to take their personal experiences 
and situate them within larger structural forces,to connect the personal 
to the political, the individual to the institutional. I also want to 
foster student autonomy, open dialogue, and independent inquiry. I will 
use fictional literature alongside sociological texts as an entry point 
into critical thinking, because stories often open doors that theory 
alone cannot.

The classroom environment I want to create is one built on respect, 
safety, and co-ownership. I want a space where every student's opinion 
is valued, where constructive feedback lifts rather than diminishes, 
and where students and I build class policies together. The tone I want 
to establish is calming yet encouraging, empathetic yet rigorous. When 
I speak of love in the classroom, as bell hooks so powerfully frames it, 
I mean kindness, inclusivity, compassion, and a genuine investment in 
each student's growth.

I also recognize that students arrive carrying lives far bigger than 
any single course. The resources I make available will go beyond 
textbooks. I intend to connect students with university mental health 
services, food assistance programs, and campus wellness activities. 
I also plan to send weekend email broadcasts, a space for motivational 
quotes, educational memes, and poetry,that remind students their 
instructor sees them as whole people.

Assessment in my classroom will reflect my belief that learning is a 
process, not a performance. Assignments will be grounded in Bloom's 
Taxonomy, moving students through levels of analysis, evaluation, and 
synthesis rather than rote recall. Weekly quizzes on assigned readings 
will keep students consistently engaged with the material. I will 
incorporate journaling as a reflective practice and free writing as a 
tool to help students access their thinking before it is shaped by the 
pressure to be right.

I am still growing as a teacher — a teacher I dreamed of becoming as 
a young person navigating a system that did not always make room for me. 
I am committed to being reflexive in my practice, continuously thinking 
about what is working, what is not, and what my students are teaching 
me in return.

My teaching philosophy is not a destination, it is a living document 
that will grow as I grow, shift as my students teach me, and deepen as 
I accumulate experience in the classroom. What will remain constant is 
the belief that first drove me to this path: everyone can learn, but 
how you pass on the information matters. I intend to honor that belief 
in every class I teach, every resource I share, and every student I 
have the privilege of standing before. I am not just teaching sociology. 
I am teaching people how to see the world differently, and that, to 
me, is everything.
